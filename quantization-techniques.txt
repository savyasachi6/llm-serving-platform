**1. RTN (Round-to-Nearest)**
RTN is the most straightforward, naive approach. It takes a high-precision weight (like FP16) and simply snaps it to the nearest available value on a lower-precision grid (like INT8 or INT4). It requires no calibration data and performs the math instantaneously.

* **How it works:** If your INT4 grid only allows values of -2, -1, 0, 1, and 2, and a weight is 1.4, RTN just rounds it down to 1.
* **Practical Example:** If you are urgently attempting to load a massive model into memory without time to run calibration scripts, you might force an on-the-fly RTN downcast. It gets the model running locally, but the language generation quality will likely degrade rapidly, especially at INT4, because critical outlier weights are permanently flattened.

**2. GPTQ (Accurate Post-Training Quantization)**
GPTQ is a heavy-duty, layer-by-layer optimization process. It uses a small dataset (e.g., 128 snippets of text) to calibrate the rounding process. When it rounds a weight and introduces an error, it calculates exactly how that error impacts the layer and adjusts the *remaining unquantized weights* to compensate.

* **How it works:** It processes weights in columns. If rounding weight A from 1.4 to 1 causes a loss of 0.4, GPTQ mathematically shifts the value of weight B to absorb that 0.4 difference, preserving the overall output of the layer.
* **Practical Example:** Downloading a pre-quantized `.safetensors` model from Hugging Face for local execution. The creator used GPTQ on a server with a calibration dataset to tightly pack the weights into 4-bit precision. When you load it via an inference engine, it runs fast with very little accuracy loss compared to the original.

**3. AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization)**
AWQ operates on the principle that weights are not created equal—some weights are significantly more important because they interact with large "outlier" activations in the data. Instead of just looking at the static weights, AWQ runs sample data through the model to see which weights actually light up the most.

* **How it works:** It finds the top ~1% of vital weights and multiplies them by a scaling factor *before* the rounding step. This protects their signal so they aren't destroyed when snapped to the INT4 grid.
* **Practical Example:** Preparing a Qwen model for local NPU inference benchmarks. By processing a few calibration texts, AWQ scales up the crucial 1% of weights. The final output is just standard INT4, meaning your Intel NPU hardware can execute it natively at maximum speed without needing special outlier-handling logic, yet the model stays highly coherent.

**4. LLM.int8()**
Unlike GPTQ or AWQ, which alter the model weights permanently on disk, LLM.int8() operates dynamically at runtime (inference). It solves the outlier problem by refusing to quantize them at all.

* **How it works:** As matrices multiply, the system identifies the columns with massive outlier values. It separates the math into two streams: the 99.9% of normal weights are multiplied in fast INT8 precision, while the 0.1% of outlier columns are calculated in high-precision FP16. The results are then merged.
* **Practical Example:** Running an unquantized PyTorch model using the `bitsandbytes` library (`load_in_8bit=True`). As the GPU processes the forward pass, it dynamically routes the heavy math through its INT8 tensor cores while carefully preserving the exact math for the outliers in FP16. It saves memory but requires more complex runtime overhead.

**5. QAT (Quantization-Aware Training)**
QAT is the most accurate but most resource-intensive method. Instead of training a model in FP16 and then trying to compress it later (Post-Training Quantization), QAT simulates the damage of quantization *during* the training or fine-tuning process itself.

* **How it works:** "Fake quantization" nodes are inserted into the training graph. During the forward pass, the weights are artificially rounded. The model calculates its loss and backpropagates errors based on those rounded values.
* **Practical Example:** Fine-tuning an agentic reinforcement learning model. By baking the rounding process into the PyTorch training loop, the neural network learns to naturally position its weights into robust zones. When the training finishes and the model is officially converted to INT8, it has already adapted to the precision loss, resulting in a model that performs almost identically to a full-precision baseline.
